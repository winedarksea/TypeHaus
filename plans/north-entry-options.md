# Catlin house north-entry options

**Original study:** 2026-09-07

**Decision update:** 2026-09-09

**Status:** decision record; the selected concept is developed in
[north-gable-extension.md](north-gable-extension.md). No model or construction changes are
authorized by this study.

## Selected direction

The subsequent gable study found a better synthesis than the three original options. The
selected north entry is now an **open, cold connector under a full-width extrusion of the
garage's south gable**, with:

- the unbuilt garage moved 2'-6" north while retaining its frost-depth ICF foundation;
- a compact shared composite upper landing around the house and garage doors;
- `SL-G-STEP-0` absorbed into `FS-BW-FLOOR`, while the interior `ST-G-SERVICE` flight stays;
- five broad tiers climbing west from an east approach; and
- a west screen or wall, with slatted on-edge 2×4s and corrugated metal as the leading
  price alternates and opal polycarbonate retained for comparison.

This is the architectural idea hiding inside the original compact-dogleg porch, but the
garage gable supplies the weather roof without a separate pavilion. It gives guests a route
that avoids the garage interior, keeps the kitchen-side yard open at grade and concentrates
the raised floor only where the two door landings need it.

The original options below remain as provenance and fallbacks. They are no longer equally
active recommendations. In particular, the old conclusion favoring the through-garage route
as the minimum-change answer is superseded.

The south porch can remain the garden/social entrance in every option. In particular, the
garage route should not be relied upon as the dwelling's required egress route: R311.1 does
not permit the required egress path to travel through a garage. The exact permit
interpretation belongs to the local official. See [Minnesota Rule
1309.0311](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/1309.0311/).

## Common requirements for any north arrival

- Establish one unmistakable pedestrian line from parking/street to the person door using
  paving, warm light, address numbers and a canopy. The overhead garage door should never
  be the strongest visual cue.
- Put roof runoff, downspouts, sliding snow and snow-storage areas outside that line. An
  entrance canopy below a metal roof is not sufficient by itself if the upper slope can
  release snow onto it.
- Use a generous, nearly level landing; a pan-flashed threshold; a replaceable scraper mat;
  high-traction wet surfaces; and an inward-opening exterior door where the detailed plan
  permits it.
- Preserve a deliberate west edge: a screen for airflow or a wall for stronger wind
  protection. Prefer a 90-degree turn before the house door. Cold-region design guidance
  identifies porches and vestibules as protection from driving rain and snow and notes the value of
  turning an entry 90 degrees; see the [U.S. Forest Service Built Environment Image Guide](https://www.fs.usda.gov/sites/default/files/fs_media/fs_document/TheBuiltEnvironmentImageGuide-2001-09.pdf).
- Keep the garage-side door weatherstripped, self-closing and fire-protective, and obtain an
  early ruling on how an enlarged connector affects garage separation. See
  [Minnesota Rule 1309.0302](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/1309.0302/).
- Make a future-access decision now. The approximately 35-inch rise would need about 35
  feet of ramp run at 1:12 before landings, so neither a short porch nor the roofed approach
  quietly becomes step-free later. Reserve space for a lift if that matters.

## Option 1 — Move the porch to the north arrival

### The broad version: not recommended

A literal porch across the north/east face sounds direct, but it creates the two problems
already identified by the owners. In the current garage position the exposed east wing is
only about six feet wide, x = 30–36 feet, and the first kitchen window straddles that edge.

**Kitchen window.** The garage blocks most of the north house wall between x = 6 and 30
feet. One kitchen window sits near that east edge and the other is farther east, so this is
precisely where a street-visible porch wants to go. The deck and a visually open 36-inch
guard are not the chief daylight problem: the kitchen glass starts about 41 inches above
the raised deck.
The damaging elements are the opaque roof, deep eave, solid screening and posts in the view
cones. That roof would remove much of the diffuse north sky and replace a yard/sky view with
a porch ceiling. A transparent roof only trades that loss for snow cover, summer gain,
visible dirt and harder flashing. A narrow skylight may soften the loss but does not restore
the view.

**Three movements on one wet platform.** The landing must absorb a five-riser exterior
stair, a house door and a garage door. If the doors remain opposite one another, their
users converge in the same four-foot zone; if either leaf swings onto it, the collision is
worse. Wind-driven snow accumulates where people must pause, unlock a door and turn.

There is also an identity problem. A large porch extending east toward the kitchen reads
like a room, but its actual destination remains the small mudroom door hidden west behind
the garage. More roof does not automatically produce clearer wayfinding.

The broad porch also occupies the present HP1 pad outside the kitchen and pushes its east
approach toward the north-facing driveway apron. It therefore creates a second conflict
between porch/stair users and vehicles even after the three-door landing is improved.

### A daylight-court version of the broad porch

If a genuine new sitting porch is important, stop the opaque connector roof at the garage's
east plane near x = 30 feet and make the kitchen portion an open-to-sky deck/light court.
Keep its guard visually open,
align narrow supports with solid wall rather than glass, put the stair at the far east edge,
and cover only its top landing with a small independent canopy. A high diffuse translucent
strip can give partial rain protection, but should not become a nominally “clear” full roof.

This saves much of the light and removes the stair from the opposing-door cell, but HP1
still moves, the route is long, and the porch/stair remains adjacent to the garage apron.
It is the best broad variant only if outdoor sitting space on the north is an objective in
its own right.

### The viable version: compact dogleg or “keyhole” porch

If this option is pursued, confine the permanent opaque roof and raised platform to the
garage-shadow zone, no farther east than x = 30 feet. Bring the exterior stair
up from the east, parallel to the walls, and give it a widened top landing before the final
westward approach to the breezeway.

```text
WEST                                                        EAST / DRIVE
solid wind wall | existing house/garage door cell | 5×6 landing | covered stair →
                    no leaf swings onto path          pause/turn
```

The key moves are:

1. **Keep the east kitchen bay open to sky.** Stop any separate porch roof at the garage's
   east plane.
   If a short canopy must cross farther east, leave a deliberate light court in front of
   both kitchen windows rather than scattering small skylights over them.
2. **Separate stair and doors.** The stair should arrive at a 5 × 6-foot turning landing,
   not terminate directly between the two doors. Both the house door and garage door should
   swing away from the porch route. If the garage door is moved, offset it several feet
   from the house door instead of preserving the face-to-face pair.
3. **Make the weather route turn.** Retain a full-height west wall and add a short north
   return, while leaving the approach open to the east. A person turns 90 degrees after the
   stair rather than walking through a west/north wind tunnel.
4. **Make the landing the porch.** One broad top tread/landing, a small wall-mounted perch,
   one sconce, house numbers and a parcel shelf are more useful here than trying to recreate
   the 18 × 8-foot south outdoor room.
5. **Give the stair a complete roof and drainage system.** The compact porch fails if the
   stairs are technically outside the roof edge or if a gutter discharges beside their
   bottom landing.

### Relative pros, cons and mitigations

| Pros | Cons | Mitigation or design response |
|---|---|---|
| Guests avoid the garage. | Broad version blocks kitchen light and view. | Keep any separate porch construction west of the garage's east plane; preserve open sky at the outer kitchen window. |
| Retains an outdoor porch character. | Stair, house door and garage door compete. | Widen the top landing; turn the stair; swing leaves away from circulation; preferably offset the garage door. |
| Uses the garage and west wall as wind shelter. | Still colder and wetter than an enclosed room. | Full stair roof, solid west/north baffle, controlled gutter and east-facing approach. |
| Can be lighter and less costly than the enclosed gallery. | The entry remains hard to see from the north until one reaches the garage corner. | Create a visible portal and address marker at the garage's southeast/east-approach corner, then repeat its light/material at the porch. |
| Leaves dirty boots outside the house. | HP3 is in the approach. | Relocate HP3 as part of the concept, not as a later field adjustment. |

**Residual downside:** after all of these mitigations, this is still an exposed stair and a
small landing serving two buildings. If full winter protection or a real bench is desired,
Option 3 is the more coherent use of the same construction zone.

## Option 2 — North door and a route through the garage

### Legacy layout: north portal plus an L-shaped clean lane

**Current-plan correction.** This option was sketched before the overhead door moved to the
north gable. The 16-foot opening now occupies x = 10–26 feet, leaving only four-foot corner
returns. A new 36-inch person door centred near x = 8 feet no longer drops harmlessly into a
blank bearing wall: it consumes nearly the whole west return and needs a coordinated corner,
header and ICF blockout redesign. The north gable wall is nonbearing, but the geometry—not a
header table—is now the constraint. This makes the route less of a “minimum-change” option
than the original comparison claimed.

Add a grade-level 36-inch insulated door to `W-G-N`, provisionally centred at global x = 8
feet only if a revised jamb/corner layout proves feasible and the opening remains clear of
the overhead-door framing. The ICF stem stands about 22 inches above the slab, so the door
still needs a preplanned blockout/grade-beam detail, jamb reinforcement and flashing. It is
work to coordinate before the ICF pour and unattractive as a retrofit.

Outside, make a 6–8-foot-wide by roughly 5–6-foot-deep entry portal with a solid west side,
an east/northeast approach, warm sconce, large address number and protected package ledge.
Add a north gutter and snow retention, and design the canopy for roof snow and drift rather
than treating it as decoration.

Inside, reserve a **42–48-inch-wide L-shaped clean lane** along the north wall, around the
hydrant, then south along the west wall to the existing service stair. The existing
geometry leaves approximately 48 inches north of the hydrant and 45½ inches west of it.
The 60 × 30-inch workbench currently consumes that northwest route and must move or fold
fully away; the heater must be coordinated and the EV cord must never cross the route.

```text
NORTH DOOR
    ↓  arrival mat / art / warm light
==== 42–48 in clean lane ===================================
    turn at protected hydrant
    ↓
==== west clean lane ====→ screened stair → glowing breezeway → tiled mudroom
                 cars, mower and closed storage stay east of this line
```

The progression should have four recognizable moments:

1. **Outdoor front door.** A good insulated door with a narrow reeded or clear vertical
   light is enough; sidelights unnecessarily enlarge both the bearing-wall and ICF opening.
2. **Small indoor threshold zone.** Give the first 5 × 6 feet a large recessed/removable mat,
   warm ceiling light, one durable art/mirror surface and no storage. It should read as a
   foyer before the parked cars become dominant.
3. **The clean lane.** Use a high-traction contrasting floor band, continuous warm light and
   a finished wall/ceiling plane. This should be a real protected zone, not painted stripes
   that objects are allowed to occupy.
4. **The destination.** Turn the unresolved guard at the 34-inch-high service-door landing
   into an intentional screen or half-wall. Highlight the stair and upgraded garage-side
   door; let the translucent breezeway glow beyond it. The final house door then opens into
   the already tiled 59-square-foot mudroom.

### Making cars, mower and storage coexist with the route

- Draw the exact two vehicle envelopes, mirrors, open doors and charging positions before
  locating the north door or cabinets. Place wheel stops so no bumper can invade the lane
  or stair. A painted line without a physical stop will eventually lose.
- After reserving the clean lane, the notional vehicle rectangle is only about 18 feet 4
  inches east–west by 18 feet 1 inch north–south before accounting for the stair, hydrant
  protection, mirrors and door swings. With even a modest one-foot stop allowance at the
  overhead door, usable vehicle length can fall to roughly 17 feet 3 inches. Ordinary
  cars/crossovers may work; long SUVs and pickups may not.
- Give the electric mower a named parking bay with its own wheel stop and charging outlet.
  If it is a riding mower or shares the garage with a snowblower, the best aesthetic
  mitigation is a small exterior equipment shed; cabinetry cannot disguise a six-foot
  machine without consuming a parking stall.
- Put only shallow 12–16-inch-deep, full-height closed cabinets on a lane wall; at the
  hydrant's roughly 4½-foot clear zone, 12-inch cabinetry still leaves about 42 inches.
  Put deep cabinets and the relocated workbench on the parking side or the south wall east
  of the stair.
- Use flush cabinet fronts and one visually quiet material. Avoid open shelves, pegboard,
  loose totes and overhead racks directly above the guest path. Store batteries/chargers in
  a ventilated manufacturer-compliant cabinet zone, not under the entry bench.
- Use parking guidance, ceiling-mounted EV cable management and door-edge protection so a
  guest is never negotiating cords, mirrors or opening car doors.

### Finish and air-quality strategy

Finish the primer-only ceiling and divide lighting into a neutral-bright work mode and a
warmer automatic entry mode. Use a scrub-resistant lower wall, closed storage and a
slip-resistant sealed-concrete or resin floor; glossy smooth coatings can become dangerous
with tracked snow and salt.

Electric vehicles remove routine tailpipe exhaust but not garage dust, road salt, tires,
stored chemicals or the possibility that combustion equipment is brought inside later.
Preserve the two-door breezeway airlock, air-seal penetrations, weatherstrip/self-close the
garage service door, share no house HVAC return, and consider an occupancy/run-on exhaust
fan. EPA's current Indoor airPLUS v2 benchmark calls for a substantially airtight
garage-to-house interface and either pressure verification or a directly exhausted 100 CFM
fan for an attached private garage; see [EPA Indoor airPLUS v2 §5.5](https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=P101B7J5.TXT).

### Relative pros, cons and mitigations

| Pros | Cons | Mitigation or design response |
|---|---|---|
| Shortest and most obvious north address. | Guests see and walk past vehicles. | Give the first threshold its own visual identity; screen lower vehicle views with cabinets/half-wall; use one deliberate light/material line to the stair. |
| Lowest relative construction scope. | Route can become storage overflow. | Make 42–48 inches a permanent no-storage zone; provide closed storage elsewhere; use wheel stops and a mower bay. |
| Very good winter protection after the first door. | New door interrupts a bearing wall and ICF stem. | Resolve it before the pour with the structural/ICF designer; avoid a later saw-cut. |
| Existing garage finish is already a strong starting point. | North eave drops rain and snow at the door. | Independent canopy, north gutter/downspout and engineered snow retention. |
| Cars and mower are electric. | “No exhaust” is not “house air.” | Keep the existing two-door airlock, improve door gaskets/closer, air-seal and optionally exhaust the garage. |
| Can look handsome without pretending to be a living room. | Large vehicles may erase the generosity. | Full-size vehicle/mirror/door mock-up is the decision gate; if 42 inches cannot remain honest, choose Option 3. |

**Residual downside:** this entrance will always be through a garage. Good architecture can
make the progression orderly, bright and dignified; it cannot make two cars and a mower
disappear. That is acceptable for a low-entertaining household only if the storage habits
and vehicle test support it.

If vehicle length is the only failure, a three- to four-foot westward garage extension is a
more honest fallback than squeezing the entry lane: it adds length along the roof ridge
rather than increasing the truss span. If storage volume is the failure, an equipment shed
or shallow annex is the direct fix. If neither is acceptable, the garage route should lose
to Option 3 rather than be narrowed into an improvised aisle.

## Option 3 — Enclosed east gallery with a flared entry lantern

### Legacy premium layout

This scheme also predates the selected 2'-6" garage move and the gable extrusion. Its old
“14-foot slot” dimensions are retained as design provenance, not current construction
coordinates. If revived, redraw it from the moved garage and current driveway before using
any dimension below.

Extend a cold, unconditioned enclosure east through the 14-foot slot and flare it into an
approximately 6 × 6-foot lantern just beyond the garage's southeast corner. Put the outer
door at its east end and keep the existing west breezeway wall fully closed.

One workable longitudinal sequence is:

```text
WEST / HOUSE DOOR                                             EAST / DRIVE
existing 4×4 cell | 3 ft top landing | 4 treads | 7+ ft lower gallery | 6×6 lantern | canopy
                  |     five 7 in risers, descending east      bench + parcel shelf  | door
```

The outside door faces east while the house and garage doors face south and north. There is
no straight west/north draft path through the house, and the stair, handrail and packages
remain dry. At the top, replace the existing east panel with an **open portal**, not a third
door. Use a continuous timber handrail or light datum that climbs west and turns south
toward the house door; give the garage-side door quieter light so the intended turn is
instinctive.

The current east post pair leaves only about 26½ inches clear and its beam is too low. This
option therefore replaces/reframes that edge and roof. It also needs a new independent
foundation strategy, HP3 relocation, movement-capable flashings to both buildings, and one
controlled roof/drainage system. It should be budgeted as an entry pavilion.

### The lantern, bench and package drop

The east flare is what changes the space from a narrow corridor into a small domestic room.
A normal 17–18-inch-deep bench in the fixed 48½-inch slot would leave only about 30 inches
of passage. In the 6 × 6-foot flare, a 4–5-foot bench can sit along the house-side wall while
preserving door maneuvering and a generous route.

Use either a wall-hung powder-coated frame with a replaceable wood/compact-laminate seat and
at least 6 inches of vacuum clearance below, or a solid coved mineral plinth with a wood
cap. Avoid open shoe cubbies, wicker baskets and inaccessible voids. If the flare cannot be
built, use a 10–12-inch-deep fold-down perch and describe the space honestly as a refined
passage.

For packages, the simple answer is an exterior 16–18-inch-deep shelf recessed into the
covered east alcove, 18–24 inches above the landing and outside the door swing. Give it a
washable back and drip edge. A lockable pass-through parcel box is possible, but its second
door, weather seals, cold bridge and insect detailing are worthwhile only if theft risk
justifies them. Giving delivery drivers smart-lock access to the whole lantern is less
robust than either solution.

### A clean, spider-resistant construction

It is unrealistic to promise a spider-free semi-exterior room. It is realistic to remove
the habitats, prey and inaccessible corners that make one unpleasant.

- Use a solid concrete/masonry stair, sealed concrete or textured exterior porcelain,
  epoxy grout and a 4–6-inch coved base. Continue the same washable material over the
  treads and risers.
- Recess a removable scraper grate or mat pan inside the east door. Pitch the lower floor
  toward a controlled east-side outlet; use a removable boot tray instead of an interior
  drain unless freeze protection, trap priming and discharge are fully resolved.
- Use a smooth ceiling and large smooth wall panels with few joints. Box beams, cap hollow
  tubes, seal penetrations and eliminate upward-facing trim, exposed rafters, slats and
  tiny ledges.
- Seal doors with replaceable compression gaskets and sweeps. Put removable fine stainless
  insect mesh at deliberate vents and drainage baffles at weeps; never block drainage in
  the name of insect control.
- Keep planting and mulch away from the enclosure; use a clean gravel or paved maintenance
  strip. Provide an accessible receptacle for a vacuum and keep all ceiling surfaces within
  reach of an extension duster.
- Keep the cold gallery able to dry through screened high/low vents, with an optional small
  humidity-controlled exhaust fan. Do not feed it intermittent warm house air, which will
  condense on cold glazing and metal.

University of Minnesota Extension recommends exclusion, reducing insect prey, removing
clutter/webs, maintaining screens and sealing gaps rather than relying on pesticides. It
also advises reducing exterior light or moving it away from a door because attracted
insects feed spiders; see [UMN Extension's Minnesota spider guidance](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/home-maintenance/household-insects/spiders).

### “Fairy lights” that belong in the architecture

The atmosphere is a good idea; loose festoon wire and exposed sockets are not. They collect
dust, dead insects and webs and look temporary beside a carefully detailed pavilion.

Use two independent layers:

- **Task/safety light:** sealed 2700 K light at the lock, both landings and stair, raised by
  an occupancy or door-contact sensor.
- **Fairy-light layer:** sparse 2200–2400 K LED points recessed behind a flush clear lens in
  a ceiling channel, or a low-output concealed LED behind a wipeable perforated-metal veil.
  Dim it, use a curfew, and let it glow through the east door without being the only light
  on the stair.

Research does not justify a simplistic “warm white always attracts fewer insects” claim:
one domestic-light field experiment found LEDs attracted fewer insects than other lamp
types but no significant difference between its warm- and cool-white LEDs. It does support
using low-output LED, limiting duration and avoiding broad blue-rich illumination. See the
[published field experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5108255/).

### Envelope and moisture strategy

Prefer a mostly opaque low-slope roof with one diffuse high strip rather than a fully clear
roof. It will be calmer, easier to clean and less prone to visible condensation. Raise or
replace the present east beam to achieve legal and comfortable headroom, then slope the new
roof to a gutter beyond the east door. Extend a roughly 3-foot canopy over the exterior
landing and discharge water away from both walk and driveway.

Use a pan-flashed threshold, water-tolerant finishes and a floor that drains or dries. WBDG
recommends weatherstripped vestibule doors together with water-resistant floors and, where
appropriate, drainage for wind-driven rain; see [WBDG's building-envelope guidance](https://stg.wbdg.org/resources/wind-safety-building-envelope).

Keep the pavilion structurally independent from the two buildings. Use slip-capable
counterflashing and compressible closures rather than rigidly foaming across structures
that can move differently, while preserving both existing rainscreen drainage paths.

### Relative pros, cons and mitigations

| Pros | Cons | Mitigation or design response |
|---|---|---|
| Cleanest and most gracious guest route. | Highest construction scope. | Keep it cold/unconditioned; use the existing walls as durable surfaces; concentrate architectural richness in the east lantern. |
| Best protection from west/NW wind, snow and rain. | Existing side opening is too narrow/low. | Replace the east frame and roof edge as planned scope; do not attempt a panel-only alteration. |
| Direct arrival into the tiled mudroom without entering garage. | Four-foot run can feel tunnel-like. | Flare to 6 × 6 feet at east; high pale ceiling; glazed door/clerestory; luminous west terminus. |
| Full bench and package drop are possible. | Bench cannot fit in the strict slot. | Put it only in the east flare; otherwise use a shallow fold-down perch. |
| Easy to clean if detailed correctly. | Cold enclosure can collect meltwater/condensation. | Mineral finishes, scraper mat, controlled drainage, coved base and screened drying path. |
| Fairy-light atmosphere can make it memorable. | Light can attract insects and create web anchors. | Recess sealed warm points behind a wipeable lens; dim, shield and curfew them; separate task lighting. |
| Leaves kitchen windows untouched. | Pedestrian route approaches the walk around the east edge of the north-facing driveway apron. | Separate it with contrasting/raised paving, light and a low protective curb, bollard or planting strip. |

**Residual downside:** this is a small building project with structural, enclosure,
mechanical, drainage and permit consequences. Its premium buys a qualitatively better
arrival, not merely nicer finishes.

## Current decision and next design work

The option selection is complete: develop the extruded-gable connector described in
[north-gable-extension.md](north-gable-extension.md). Its next package must resolve the
following before the house model changes:

1. **Survey and zoning:** confirm the garage 2'-6" north, driveway grades, full roof
   projection, lot coverage and whether Saint Paul treats the near-touching roof as attached.
2. **Excavation/foundation section:** retain frost-depth garage ICF; verify the garage south
   footing bears on competent native soil or specified engineered fill; fix the house-first,
   garage-before-backfill sequence.
3. **Roof structure:** obtain a truss-engineer concept and price for the full-width six-foot
   gable cantilever, including drift, uplift, fire/draft blocking and a pile-supported fallback.
4. **Shared landing:** draw every bearing for `FS-BW-FLOOR`, its continuation through the
   garage door, the retirement of `SL-G-STEP-0`, threshold drainage and differential movement.
5. **Tiered approach:** fix five equal rises, four broad treads, lower landing, handrail,
   guards, snow storage and HP3 relocation.
6. **West edge alternates:** price and mock up the on-edge 2×4 screen and corrugated-metal
   windwall on the same elevation; keep opal polycarbonate as the additive alternate.

The through-garage route remains the least exterior construction only if its north-door and
vehicle-clearance test can be solved. The enclosed gallery remains the premium fallback if a
fully weather-sealed arrival later becomes more important than cost.
